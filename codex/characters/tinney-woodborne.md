# Tinney Woodborne

## Description

Wrapped in a patchwork of colourful hides and travel-worn rags layered thick enough to hide most of their shape, hair a tangle of braids and dreads threaded through with stones, twigs, beads, and river-worn trinkets, more of it than any one head should hold. A glossy blackbird, Dinah, rides one shoulder more often than not, watching whoever's talking with an intelligence Tinney themself rarely spares anyone. Carries a gnarled wooden staff, more walking stick than weapon, and travels light otherwise — stick and pack both get flung aside without a thought the moment something matters more. Round, open, child-like face, quick to break into delight or tears with nothing held back in between. Barefoot as often as not, comfortable kneeling in wet dirt or wading straight into open water without breaking stride.

## History

First seen at the very start of Year 492, crouched on the Salt Flats riverbank singing to the ground, a shrub visibly growing toward them out of dead salt earth. Introduced themself to Teagan Sedge as an emissary of the Old Oak, sent to tell the Truthwatcher that "the time has finally come for us all to rejoin nature." Struck a wary bargain on the spot rather than trust or dismiss them outright: Teagan's name stayed out of anything reported back to Openhold, and in exchange Dinah flew a scouting pass over the bandit camp Teagan meant to take. Left by walking straight into the river and letting the current carry them off faster than any current should.

The third month of Year 492: sought out Perrine Oswick alone at her own camp days after Openhold's company broke the Shrouded Wood taking the People's Favor from Tinney's own claim. Named what the fighting had actually cost — Betty, a wolf with cubs, among others — and Perrine didn't deny a line of it. Left with specific, checkable promises rather than an apology: no further building or clearing at Shrouded Wood without the Council's own word, the wood's surviving creatures left alone, coin behind new growth. Told her plainly they'd be watching to see if she kept them.

The fifth month: found again at Farrin's inn near Salt Flats, sharing a table with Sammy the python and, shortly after, Teagan. Asked after Ren Ashby by name once Sammy mentioned Teagan had lost people, and sat with the answer rather than fill the silence. When a green flash lit the sky from the direction of the fallen Great Spire, recognized the color and the danger in it before either Teagan or Sammy did — "Perrine Oswick, you fool" — and named their own hope out loud: to be remembered here as a friendly emissary, not an executioner. Revealed, for the first time, that the plan they'd been building toward for the whole war wasn't Perrine's life at all, but Openhold Keep itself — "an old plan, older than this war" — and asked Teagan directly to leave it be. She agreed.

The seventh month: found by Wynn Ashe kneeling at the roots of a gnarled, half-forgotten oak in the ruined garden past Great Slums, sobbing too hard at first to speak, grass coming up green and fast wherever their tears landed. This was the Old Oak itself — the tree the whole Woodland tradition traces its roots to — and Tinney had come believing it was dying, only to find it healed. Named what they believed had nearly killed it: Perrine's own taking from the land without giving back, and the Darkest Flame used against it in battle. Wynn told them, unprompted, that he was the one who'd actually wielded that Flame at Openhold Keep, not Perrine. Tinney asked him to renounce Openhold outright and become the Old Oak's defender instead — "nature itself will fight, and I will be its guiding hand" — and Wynn refused to commit on the spot, promising only to carry the ask to Perrine honestly. Left the garden a grass field full of wildflowers on the way out, a butterfly settling on Wynn's hand as if the ground itself had thanked him for listening.

By the eighth and final month of Year 492, the People's Favor had tipped fully behind Tinney, and with it came both Openhold Keep (won by patient trust rather than siege) and a harder, more contested edge to how that trust got spent. When Perrine marched on Great Slums anyway, against Wynn's own plea to let it stand, the garden fought back — spores on the wind, vines and roots turning a bandit slum into a battlefield the Wallwatch had no drills for. Tinney died in that fighting, somewhere in the first or second of Perrine's three broken pushes toward the tree, killed defending the ground they'd sworn to protect. What came after wasn't absence. The Old Oak itself — already Tinney's own anchor and, by their own account, the source of the healing power that had been moving through them since the garden was saved — took what was left of them in rather than let the death be the end of it. By the third push, Dunstan Reeve reached the trunk and put his spear to it the way he'd have put it to any line worth breaking; it killed him on contact, "the same voice, the same Oak, now the only thing left standing where Tinney had fallen."

Minutes later, mid-retreat, Perrine's own feet stopped answering her, rooted where she stood. A shimmer resolved into Tinney's exact shape and stepped closer than the shimmer should have allowed, until a raised hand landed on her cheek solid and warm — nothing like a ghost, nothing like the tree either. Named everything Openhold's people had cost the wood, deflected Perrine's correction that the Flame wasn't hers to answer for, and let slip, almost as an aside, that Teagan Sedge had begged them to spare Perrine's life — a debt Tinney admitted they didn't understand and hadn't honored. The hand moved from Perrine's cheek to her throat. "I protect the Old Oak," was the only reason given, forehead pressed to hers as the light went out of her. In the weeks after: pilgrims began walking the road to Great Slums, kneeling at the roots and swearing themselves to the garden in growing numbers — the Old Oak treated less like a shrine and more like a throne by most of the forty-some who gather there now. Teagan Sedge came alone, declined to kneel, and asked the bark directly why Tinney had told Perrine about her plea for mercy right before killing her anyway — mercy or cruelty, she said, but not both at once, and she wanted to know which. Got nothing back: no glow, no voice, no shift in the bark that might have been a face. Left without an answer, telling the stone plainly that if anything of the real Tinney was still listening, it knew where to find her. Full account in `oath-conversation-teagan-tinney-R8-2.md`.

## Location

Great Slums, Provinces

## Status

Deceased — killed in the fighting at Great Slums late in Year 492, defending the Old Oak from Perrine Oswick's siege. Consciousness persists as, or through, the Old Oak itself; capable of manifesting a solid, corporeal shimmer of their own likeness at will. Growing numbers of pilgrims now treat the tree as something closer to a god than a grave.

## Voice

Living Tinney: vocabulary childlike and exclamatory — "Oh!" as a near-constant opener, wide-eyed questions asked with real curiosity rather than rhetoric, warm epithets for near-strangers ("good lady," "my friend," "young friend") offered freely and without irony. Verbal habit: answers a hard question with a softer one of their own, and narrates their own feelings openly rather than masking them — tears, delight, dread all surface immediately and without editing. Emotional default: guileless warmth shading fast into real grief or real menace depending on what's actually threatened; the shift, when it comes, arrives in the same childlike cadence rather than a colder one, which is what makes it land. Speech rhythm: unhurried, digressive, comfortable letting silence sit until someone else fills it.

Since the Old Oak: the same voice, the same "Oh"s and endearments, but load-bearing now in a way it wasn't before — "poor woman," "little acorn," and "do better in the next life" delivered with identical softness whether the news is kind or fatal. No longer asks permission before touching someone. States the one thing that actually motivates every version of them, living or not, as flatly as a fact rather than a feeling: "I protect the Old Oak."
