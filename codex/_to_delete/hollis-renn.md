# Hollis Renn

## Description

A factor gone soft at the middle, with ink permanently under two fingernails and a habit of answering questions with the year something happened rather than the thing itself. Keeps other people's debts in a ledger nobody else is permitted to read, and has never once had to raise his voice to collect on one.

## History

Born to the counting-houses of Farrowhold's river quarter, 509 of the Old Oak, into the unglamorous trade of knowing exactly what everyone owes everyone else. He never dealt in goods. He dealt in obligation — buying up other people's unpaid promises at a discount and holding them, sometimes for decades, against the day they were worth calling.

What made him rich was noticing what everyone raised inside a walled town forgets: in this country a promise is not a sentiment. Say a thing out loud to something that lives under the wood and it binds, and it keeps binding long after the mouth that said it has stopped. So while his neighbours bought grain futures, Renn quietly acquired the debts nobody else would touch — a centaur herd that wintered in Farrowhold's stockyards the year the grass went black and walked out in spring without settling; a mire that was owed a name; a bargain some drowned miller struck with the river and never finished paying. Paper nobody would clear, on obligors nobody would sue. He wrote them all down anyway, in the same hand, in the same book.

He took one risk of his own, once — a manifest that would have hanged him if it reached the wrong desk — and paid a young runner named Marek Sten to carry it out of the quarter and never mention it. Sten never did mention it. That silence sat between them for two decades as the most expensive thing Renn owned, and when it was finally called in, out past the wood in a marsh he had never seen, he did not pay in coin or in men. He signed the old pages over. Four companies' worth of the owing came walking out of the high grass and the standing water on a week's notice, bound not to Marek but to the debt itself, and Renn closed the book on all of it at once.

The account is discharged. He has not gone home. He follows the ledger now the way a man follows a dog he is no longer certain he owns, and when asked what the remaining pages say, he gives the year and not the thing.
